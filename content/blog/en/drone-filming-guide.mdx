Aerial shots have become close to essential in many kinds of video. They show the scale of a property, the context of a location and the progress of a project in a way a ground camera simply can't. At the same time, drone filming is more than "put the drone up and shoot". There are rules, limits and conditions that affect both what is possible and how good the result looks.

Here is what is worth knowing before you book a drone shoot.

## When drone footage is worth it

### Real estate

For houses, villas, plots and residential developments, aerial images show what you can't see from the street: the shape of the land, the neighbourhood, access roads, the distance to a forest, a lake or the city centre. One well-chosen aerial shot can answer questions a buyer would otherwise ask on the phone.

### Construction progress

Repeated flights from the same points at regular intervals document how a site evolves. That is useful for investors, internal reporting and the developer's marketing. At the end, the footage can be cut into a timelapse of the whole project.

### Events

At weddings, festivals and corporate events, a drone gives you striking opening shots and wide views of the venue. This is where the rules on flying over people matter most, so the shot list is planned around them.

### Tourism and hospitality

Guesthouses, hotels, restaurants with a view, wineries and attractions: a drone puts the place in context and conveys the feel of the area. It is one of the most effective types of content for promoting a destination.

### Ads and company videos

For factories, warehouses, farms or industrial parks, an aerial shot shows the scale of the business better than any text. Built into a company video or a commercial, it adds production value you can see.

## EU drone rules in brief

Since 2021, drone flights in EU member states, Romania included, have mainly been governed by a common European framework, overseen at EU level by EASA (the European Union Aviation Safety Agency) and applied nationally by each country's authority. In Romania, that authority is AACR, the Romanian Civil Aeronautical Authority.

The general principles:

### Operating categories

- **Open category.** Covers most low-risk flights: within the pilot's visual line of sight, at a limited height and with strict rules on distance from people. It is split into subcategories based on the drone's weight and how close it flies to people.
- **Specific category.** For higher-risk operations that don't fit the open category. It requires a risk assessment and, as a rule, an authorisation from the national authority.
- **Certified category.** For high-risk operations comparable to conventional aviation. Not relevant to normal commercial filming.

### Operator registration

In most cases, drone operators must register with the national authority, especially when the drone carries a camera or exceeds a certain weight. The operator number is displayed on the drone. Pilots also need the theoretical training required for the category they fly in.

### Flying over people

This is one of the strictest rules. In the open category, flying over assemblies of people is not allowed, and flying over uninvolved people is limited or prohibited depending on the subcategory. In practice, at an event with guests, shots are planned so the drone does not pass over the crowd, and the people involved in the shoot are informed.

### Restricted zones

Airspace is not open everywhere. There are zones where flying is prohibited or allowed only with approval, for example:

- around airports and airfields;
- near military or strategic sites;
- above certain urban areas or institutions, where temporary or permanent restrictions may apply;
- in protected natural areas, which may have additional rules.

## How it works in Romania

In Romania, the airspace status of each location is checked before every flight. Information on restricted zones and coordination procedures is published by the competent authorities, AACR and ROMATSA (the Romanian Air Traffic Services Administration). In some zones, flights can only take place after obtaining approval or coordinating with air traffic services. Some locations may also carry additional requirements for aerial filming or photography.

For you as a client, this means:

- share the exact address of the location as early as possible;
- in some areas, the shoot may need extra preparation time;
- in certain places, flying may not be possible at all, in which case we look for an alternative (a different take-off point, another angle, or ground-level filming).

## Weather: the factor nobody controls

Drones are sensitive to the weather. The main factors are:

- **Wind.** Strong gusts affect image stability and flight safety. Even if it feels calm on the ground, the wind a few dozen metres up can be much stronger.
- **Rain and snow.** Most drones are not designed for precipitation. The flight gets postponed.
- **Fog and low visibility.** Beyond image quality, the flight has to stay within the pilot's line of sight.
- **Cold.** Low temperatures shorten battery life, and with it the actual filming time.

That is why we usually agree a backup date for drone shoots. Postponing by a day beats delivering unsafe or poor-quality footage.

## Getting the light right

Time of day completely changes how aerial footage looks:

- **Golden hour**, just after sunrise and before sunset, gives warm light, long shadows and a cinematic feel. Ideal for real estate, tourism and ads.
- **Midday**, with the sun high, gives strong colours but harsh shadows. Useful for documenting sites or when crisp detail matters.
- **Light overcast** gives even light, good for large surfaces and technical photos.
- **Blue hour**, after sunset, works very well for lit buildings, hotels and restaurants.

When we plan a shoot, we take into account which way the building faces, so the main facade is well lit.
